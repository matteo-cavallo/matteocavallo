Living in Paris has a specific feeling. Not the postcard one — the other one. The one where you wake up, commute, work, come home, and somewhere between the cracks of the daily routine you realize you haven't really been there.

---

That's what I call autopilot. Not laziness. Not unhappiness. Just absence. The lights are on, you're functional from the outside, but your inner self is gone.

I think it happens more when you're far from home. Not because home is perfect — it isn't. But home is full of mirrors. Family, places, language, people who've known you long enough to reflect something back. When you move to another city, the mirrors break and you're left with your raw image of yourself.

Paris doesn't know who I was at nineteen. It only knows who I am now. Sometimes that's liberating. Most times, it's disorienting. It forces you to build the idea of yourself again from scratch. And it's so easy to build a fake version of yourself — the one that doesn't belong to you, a reflection of something far from what is inside you.

Lynch knew this. In [Mulholland Drive](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulholland_Drive), a woman constructs a version of herself so complete, so convincing, that she forgets the original. [Then a blue box opens.](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4b_WYKhDRkc)

When it opens, reality takes over. You face the unfiltered version of the life you've built — around expectations, desires, years of accumulated wanting. Two things become clear at once: how much you've grown, how many new people and places now make up who you are. And how many versions of yourself don't exist anymore.

There's tension between the person you want to become and the person you think you are. It's a constant work, a daily practice, an art you return to every day.

Self-critique kicks in around this point — the gap between who I am and who I thought I was going to become. I'm learning not to treat it as a crisis. It's a signal. The fact that I'm still asking the question means I haven't stopped paying attention.

The tension doesn't resolve. But it means something is still alive in there.

What I've found, slowly, is that the answer isn't resolving the tension. It's staying present to the meaningful — not to everything, just to what's real.
